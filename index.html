What happens to the text

Characters

’ ‘ ‚ ‛ ‹ › ′ ´ ʼ become '; “ ” „ ‟ « » ″ become ". A backtick ` also becomes ' — the reverse replacement is not in the program at all. – — ‒ ― − ‐ ‑ become a plain hyphen, … becomes three dots, every kind of Unicode space becomes an ordinary one, zero-width characters and soft hyphens are dropped, and ¿ ¡ are deleted. Diacritics are removed through Unicode decomposition, so every caron, acute, grave, umlaut, circumflex, tilde, cedilla, ring, macron, breve, ogonek and dot above is covered. Letters without a decomposition are handled by table: ß→ss, Ǽ→AE, Æ Œ Ø Ł Đ Þ ı İ. With German selected the umlauts are spelled out instead: ä ö ü become ae oe ue, a capital one keeps only its first letter capital (Ärger → Aer-ger) and turns fully capital only inside a word already in capitals (ÄPFEL → AEP-FEL), so Mädchen reads Maedchen and hören reads hoeren. Each pair still counts as a single vowel when the word is split, which keeps Kue-che and Traeu-me where Kü-che and Träu-me break.

Deletions and rewrites

Square brackets go with their contents ([Intro], [Guitar Solo]) when that box is ticked, and so do round ones ((Oh), (Yeah)). Untick the backing vocals and only the round brackets go, since the words inside are still sung: (Oh) don't go → Oh don't go. Roman numbering at the start of a line goes too (I., IV., VII.); elsewhere it is kept, so a sentence ending in I. survives. A line left empty by a deletion is removed rather than left blank. The recommendation block Genius pastes into lyrics — a You might also like line followed by up to three title-and-artist pairs — is always removed. A group only counts as a pair when it is exactly two lines fenced by blank lines, both short, free of sentence punctuation and not opening with the same word, with the second line starting on a capital the way an artist name does; anything else is left alone, so a couplet of your own survives. Mr. and Mrs. are spelled out as M-r. and M-rs. Contractions are left exactly as they are: don't, y'all, it's, I'm. A one-letter W or V, S, Z or K is joined to the next word by a plus, the way Slavic prepositions are sung: W półlitrówce → W+pol-li-trow-ce, z czterech → z+czte-rech, s nimi → s+ni-mi. This only happens with Russian, Ukrainian or Polish selected — elsewhere a lone letter is a name or an initial, not a preposition, and is left alone.

Italian and Spanish join two words with a plus where they are sung on one note: a word ending in a vowel followed by a word beginning with one — pi-sa un → pi-sa+un, tutto il → tut-to+il, mi amor → mi+a-mor. A silent h is no barrier (l'ho+am-mo-scia-ta, la+ho-ra), while a stressed vowel on either side or a comma between the words keeps them apart — in Italian only, since Spanish sings qué hacer joined as que+ha-cer. Spanish hie- hua- words stay separate too, and an accented í or ú breaks the diphthong inside a word: dí-a, ve-í-as, fan-ta-sí-as. French works the other way: the hiatus is kept, and what joins is the liaison after a short function word — les+a-mis, en+hi-ver, tres+u-tile, ils+ont — but never after et or before an aspirate h (les he-ros, en haut).

Existing hyphens

A hyphen between letters or digits becomes a space: hip-hop → hip hop, 24-7 → 24 7. A dash standing on its own between words is dropped along with one of its spaces: she said - it's over → she said it's over.

Syllables

Hyphens are only ever inserted. Before a word is returned it is checked against the original with the hyphens removed — if anything else changed, the word is left alone. So no letter is ever dropped, added or moved: y'all never turns into yall. The one exception is the elision table in the script: a spelling like mem'ry is one syllable short of memory on purpose, so it is rewritten in full and divided the way it is sung — memo-ry, while a plain memory stays me-mo-ry. That table is a fixed list of words, never a guess.

The rules are tuned for English. Pick another language and the English-specific parts switch off — the -ed/-es/-ing/-ly/-gue/n't endings, the compound and exception tables — while the vowel and consonant handling changes with it. A final -e counts as a syllable everywhere except English and French. Each language keeps its own digraphs whole: cz sz rz dz szcz prz for Polish, sch for German, gn for French, ll rr for Spanish, gn gl sc gh for Italian, ng ny for Bahasa Indonesia and Tagalog, zh kh ts shch for Russian and Ukrainian. Spanish keeps s at the end of a syllable (es-tre-lla) while Italian opens the next one with it (que-sto).

Korean, Japanese, Bahasa Indonesia and Tagalog are handled from their romanized spelling. For Japanese every vowel is a mora and gets its own note, so each one becomes a syllable (a-ri-ga-to-u, ka-wa-i-i) and y is always a consonant (kyo-u). Korean keeps the Revised Romanization vowel pairs whole (eo eu ae oe ui) and lets ng close a syllable (sa-rang-hae). Indonesian and Tagalog split every vowel pair except their own diphthongs (sau-da-ra, ba-hay).

Where the rules are unsure they under-split rather than risk a wrong break, so a few words come back whole (tired, hundreds of exceptions aside). Two tables near the top of the script hold everything the rules cannot work out — WORD_EXCEPTIONS for single words, COMPOUNDS for compounds and ER_NOT_SUFFIX for words that merely happen to end in -er; adding a line to any of them is enough, and the -s/-es/-ed/-ing/-ly/-ness forms follow automatically. Words read both ways — blessed, learned, cursed, aged — are left whole on purpose.
